Session 2: Introduction to PsychoPy

From Python in the console to stimuli on the screen

Welcome to the second session of our Python and PsychoPy workshop! Let’s take stock of what you can already do. In Session 1, you went from print("Hello world!") to writing your own loops. Along the way, you learned how to:

  • store and inspect values in variables, and tell your data types apart (strings, integers, floats);
  • take strings apart with indexing, slicing, and methods like .split() and .upper();
  • build and mutate lists: index them, append to them, even nest them;
  • repeat code with for and while loops, counters included;
  • make code decide with relational and logical operators and if/elif/else;
  • (we ran out of time for functions: the chapter and exercise are online; we’ll pick the concept up when the experiment gives us a reason to).

This is genuinely all the Python you need to run a sophisticated experiment. Today you’ll see why: a real experiment is a list of stimuli, a loop over trials, an if/else for feedback, and an f-string on the screen.

By the end of today, you will have built a working experiment that shows images in random order, collects keyboard responses, and gives feedback on every trial.

Today, we change environment: from UCloud to the PsychoPy Coder on your own laptop. This “Coder” is a simpler editor bundled with PsychoPy, and the Python inside it is the same Python you already know.

Learning outcomes

After this session, you can:

  • Open and close a PsychoPy window and explain the draw-flip cycle (prepare, draw, flip) that gives experiments their precise timing.
  • Present text and image stimuli (TextStim, ImageStim), for a fixed duration (core.wait()) or until a keypress (event.waitKeys()).
  • Write your own functions with def and parameters, and use them to remove repetition from an experiment script.
  • Load stimulus files automatically with glob.glob() instead of typing filenames by hand.
  • Randomise trial order with random.shuffle() and explain why randomisation matters.
  • Collect and restrict keyboard responses (keyList=['left', 'right']), evaluate them against a rule read from the filename, and give conditional feedback.
  • Measure reaction times with core.Clock(): reset at stimulus onset, read at the response.

How today works

Today alternates between two modes. In short lecture segments, we introduce each concept and demonstrate it live on the projector; in the exercises you put the concepts to work on your own, at your own pace, with the teachers circulating. The chapters on this site follow the same order as the lectures, so everything shown at the front is here to re-read.
